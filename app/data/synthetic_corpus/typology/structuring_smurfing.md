# Typology: Structuring / Smurfing

**Document type:** Typology
**Status:** Synthetic placeholder — illustrative only, not real compliance guidance.

## Description

Structuring, sometimes called smurfing, is the practice of breaking a
large sum of money into multiple smaller transactions specifically to
stay under reporting or scrutiny thresholds. The underlying funds may be
entirely illicit in origin, or the structuring itself may be an attempt
to avoid attention regardless of source. The defining feature is not the
size of any single transaction but the deliberate pattern designed to
avoid detection.

## Common Patterns

- Multiple cash deposits, each just under a reporting threshold, made
  across different branches, ATMs, or short time windows by the same
  individual or closely associated individuals.
- A single beneficial owner directing several individuals ("smurfs") to
  each deposit or transfer a portion of a larger sum, so no one
  transaction or one person's activity appears large.
- Rapid sequences of transactions timed to avoid appearing as a single
  aggregated amount within a monitoring window.
- Use of multiple accounts, sometimes at different institutions, to
  fragment what is functionally one flow of funds.

## Red Flags

- Deposit amounts that cluster suspiciously close to, but consistently
  under, a known reporting threshold.
- Multiple related parties making similar-sized deposits into the same
  account or into accounts that quickly consolidate funds.
- A customer whose declared income or business activity does not
  plausibly explain the frequency or aggregate volume of small
  transactions observed.
- Sudden onset of structuring-like patterns in an account with no prior
  similar history.

## Investigative Approach

An investigator should aggregate transactions across a meaningful window
(not just look at individual transactions in isolation), map any related
parties or accounts involved, and compare the aggregate pattern against
the customer's stated business or personal profile. Timing clusters
(e.g., several deposits within the same day or week, just under
threshold) are often more telling than any single transaction's amount.
