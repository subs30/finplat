# Typology: Funnel Accounts / Rapid Fund Movement

**Document type:** Typology
**Status:** Synthetic placeholder — illustrative only, not real compliance guidance.

## Description

A funnel account is an account that receives deposits from many different
sources or locations — often geographically dispersed — and then
transfers the aggregated funds elsewhere in a small number of larger
transactions. The pattern is common in cash-based fraud and drug
proceeds movement, where many small, geographically scattered cash
deposits are consolidated into one account before being moved onward as
a smaller number of larger transfers, similar in spirit to structuring
but organized around consolidation rather than fragmentation.

## Common Patterns

- Deposits made in-person or via ATM across many different cities,
  branches, or regions, inconsistent with the account holder's stated
  home location or normal activity radius.
- A high ratio of distinct depositing locations to the account holder's
  plausible personal travel or business footprint.
- Funds consolidated and moved onward via wire transfer or check shortly
  after reaching a threshold balance, then the cycle repeats.
- Account holder profile (e.g., a private individual with no multi-city
  business) inconsistent with the geographic spread of deposit activity.

## Red Flags

- Deposits from cities or regions the account holder has no documented
  connection to, occurring repeatedly rather than as an isolated
  incident.
- A consistent cycle of many small deposits followed by one larger
  outbound transfer, repeating on a regular interval.
- Deposits made by third parties on behalf of the account holder across
  multiple locations, when the relationship between depositor and
  account holder is unclear or unverified.

## Investigative Approach

Mapping deposit geography against the account holder's known profile is
the fastest way to surface a funnel account — a normal individual or
small local business rarely has deposit activity spread across many
distant locations. Once geographic dispersion is confirmed, an
investigator should examine the outbound side of the cycle to identify
where the consolidated funds ultimately go, since that destination is
often the more useful lead.
