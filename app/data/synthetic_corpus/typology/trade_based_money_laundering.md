# Typology: Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)

**Document type:** Typology
**Status:** Synthetic placeholder — illustrative only, not real compliance guidance.

## Description

Trade-based money laundering uses international trade transactions —
invoices, shipping documents, letters of credit — to move value across
borders while disguising it as ordinary commercial activity. The
mechanism typically relies on misrepresenting the price, quantity, or
quality of goods so that the difference between the true value and the
invoiced value functions as a hidden transfer of funds between the
parties.

## Common Patterns

- Over-invoicing: goods invoiced at a price well above genuine market
  value, so the buyer effectively transfers excess value to the seller
  disguised as a payment for goods.
- Under-invoicing: goods invoiced at a price well below genuine market
  value, allowing value to be retained or transferred in the destination
  jurisdiction outside formal payment channels.
- Phantom shipments: trade documentation for goods that are never
  actually shipped, or shipped in quantities inconsistent with the paper
  trail.
- Multiple invoicing: the same shipment invoiced more than once to
  justify multiple payments.
- Complex routing of goods or documentation through jurisdictions with
  limited customs transparency, similar in spirit to jurisdictional
  layering in fund transfers.

## Red Flags

- Invoiced prices that are significantly inconsistent with publicly
  observable market prices for the same goods.
- Shipping documentation that does not match the type, weight, or volume
  of goods described on the invoice.
- Payment amounts and timing that do not align with the trade
  documentation's stated terms.
- Repeated use of intermediary trading companies with no clear operating
  history or physical presence, positioned between the actual buyer and
  seller.
- A pattern of trade activity that is economically illogical (e.g.,
  consistently paying above-market prices with no apparent business
  justification).

## Investigative Approach

TBML review typically requires cross-referencing trade documentation
(invoices, bills of lading, letters of credit) against independent price
and shipping benchmarks, since the fraud lives in the gap between
documented and true value rather than in the payment mechanics alone.
