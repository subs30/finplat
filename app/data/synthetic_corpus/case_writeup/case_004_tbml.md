# Case Write-up: CASE-2025-0301 — Over-Invoiced Electronics Trade

**Document type:** Case write-up
**Status:** Synthetic placeholder — fictional case, not a real investigation.

## Summary

A trade-finance review flagged a series of letters of credit supporting
imports of consumer electronics components, where the invoiced unit price
was significantly above comparable market pricing for the same
component category.

## Observed Activity

Over a five-month period, the importer processed eleven separate
shipments of the same generic electronic component, each invoiced at
roughly 2.5 to 3 times the price observed in independent trade-price
benchmarks for comparable goods of the stated specification. Shipping
documentation confirmed goods were physically received in quantities
consistent with the invoices, ruling out a phantom-shipment pattern — the
mismatch was specifically in price, not existence or quantity of goods.

## Analysis

This is consistent with the over-invoicing pattern within the
trade-based money laundering typology: the importer's payments to the
overseas supplier included a substantial premium over genuine market
value, which functions as a value transfer disguised as a legitimate
trade payment. The importer's stated justification — that the component
was a "premium specification" variant — did not hold up against the
trade documentation, which described a standard, undifferentiated
specification matching the benchmarked comparison goods.

Further review found the overseas supplier had been incorporated only
slightly before the first flagged shipment and shared a registered
address with several other trading entities, raising a secondary shell
company concern about the counterparty itself.

## Disposition

The case was escalated jointly to trade-finance compliance and
suspicious activity review. A report was filed, and the letter-of-credit
facility for this importer was suspended pending further review of the
counterparty relationship.

## Lessons Noted

Ruling out a phantom-shipment explanation early (goods really were
received) helped focus the investigation specifically on price
manipulation rather than existence-of-goods questions, which sharpened
the benchmarking work that ultimately substantiated the finding.
